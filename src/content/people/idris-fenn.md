## The crime

Idris was a film editor with an unlawful gift for making footage tell a kinder
story than the one it recorded. Hired to tidy security tapes, he learned to lift
a handful of frames from a timeline so that a man never crossed a room, a hand
never rose, a minute simply never happened. His cuts were invisible, which was
the whole offence; evidence is meant to be the one recording nobody edits.

## The sentence

A colourist noticed the timecode skip a breath it should not have skipped, and
the invisible cut became very visible indeed. He served two years. He has said
since that the worst of it was professional: he had done his finest work on the
one thing that most needed to remain untouched.

## What the course subtracted

Inside, the cut became honest again. He teaches that editing is the discipline's
most literal example, because a cut is a material fact you can point to,
timestamped and defensible, rather than a deletion you hope no one finds. He now
runs the seminar's practice weeks and marks the two studio-based assessments,
holding, as ever, that a good cut should be uncomfortable to make and only
slightly less uncomfortable to defend, and that you should always be able to
show the frame where it happened.
